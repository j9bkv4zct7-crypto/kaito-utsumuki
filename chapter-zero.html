Prologue: Five Assholes and a Student

Hi. I'm Kaito. I'm twenty. I'm very smart and I'm very tired, and those two things are related.

I know how that sounds. People who say they're smart usually aren't, and people who say they're tired usually just stayed up playing games. I did neither. I was lying on my back in a dorm room at 6:12 in the morning with my eyes open, watching the rain run down the window in long, stupid, perfectly predictable lines, and I had not slept. Not a minute. I'd spent the night rewriting a presentation that was already finished, because the first version had one slide where the font was off by half a point, and if I couldn't fix it I couldn't breathe.

That's the kind of tired I mean.

My phone went off on the desk. A cheerful marimba tune. The same one every morning, because I don't like surprises, and a new alarm sound is a surprise.

For about two seconds, nothing happened. The room was quiet. The rain kept doing its thing.

Then, from somewhere behind my eyes:

"Up. Now. Shoes. Move."

"Five more minutes, babe," something muffled said, in the tone of a man being pulled out of a very warm bath.

"Technically," a third voice began, "you are ninety-nine percent empty space. The atoms in your body—"

"Is it safe?" a fourth one cut in, small and wobbly. "Is it safe to get up? Did anyone check? What if the floor's gone?"

"Don't ruin it," said the fifth, dreamy and aggrieved. "I was having a great dream. There was a girl, and she had these—"

"Everybody," I said, out loud, to the ceiling, "shut up. Please."

They did. For about four seconds.

That's them. The five. Nobody else hears them. I made them up when I was nine, and they never left.

I should explain that, because the next part of my life makes no sense without it, and honestly the part after that makes even less.

I have Asperger's. I was diagnosed when I was eight, in a beige office with a box of tissues on every surface, by a woman who kept asking me how I felt about things and kept looking disappointed when I answered with facts. The word isn't in fashion anymore. The doctors have folded it into a bigger word with a spectrum attached. But it was the word they gave me when I was a kid, and it's the one I grew up inside, so it's the one I use. It isn't a disease. I'm not broken. My brain runs a different operating system, and most of the world is software written for a different one.

Here is what that means in practice, since you're going to be spending a lot of time inside my head and it helps to know the layout.

I don't read faces fast. I read them the way you'd read a language you learned from a textbook: I can get there, but it takes a second, and by then the other person has already moved on to the next sentence. I take things literally. When someone says "we should totally hang out sometime," I check my calendar. I don't understand why you'd say it if you didn't mean it, and I'm usually the only one in the room who's confused when it turns out you didn't. Loud rooms hurt. Not figuratively. A crowded cafeteria is a wall of noise with edges, and every fork on every plate has its own opinion. I can talk for six hours straight about a topic I love, and I can't manage six seconds of small talk about the weather. Weather is a thing everyone already knows. Why would you say it out loud?

And I'm smart. I'm not going to be coy. I'm smart in the way that makes teachers tell your parents you're "gifted" and then, in the next breath, "but he doesn't really engage with the other children." I finished high school at sixteen. I started university at seventeen. I was the youngest person in every room, and the strangest, and I was usually both at the same time.

Being smart in a body that can't make friends is a weird, specific kind of lonely. You can solve a differential equation in your head, and you can't figure out why the girl at the bus stop is angry at you. The first one is hard. The second one is impossible. The second one is also the only one anyone cares about.

So when I was nine years old, I did the only thing I could think of. I built help.

TIM

Tim was first.

It was a thunderstorm. I was nine, and my parents were at a work dinner, and the babysitter had fallen asleep on the couch with the television on. Thunder in the apartment is a different animal than thunder outside. It comes through the walls and the floor. It comes through your teeth. I was under my bed with a pillow over my head, counting, because counting helps, and somewhere around the eleventh crack of thunder I realized I wasn't counting alone.

"Is it going to hit us?" someone whispered.

I didn't answer. I assumed I was hearing the babysitter.

"I think it's going to hit us. I think the lightning is looking for the building."

"Lightning doesn't look for things," I whispered back, and then I stopped, because the babysitter was in the other room and the voice was not in the other room. It was in me.

The voice said, "But what if it does?"

I want to tell you I was scared. I wasn't, at least not of the voice. For the first time that night, the fear had somewhere to go. Instead of one nine-year-old holding a whole storm in his chest, now there was a second person who was also holding it, and the weight split in half.

I named him Tim because it sounded like a kid who'd cry at a funeral for a goldfish, and that was exactly right. He was terrified of everything. And that night, around the fifteenth crack of thunder, he said: "Move. Move away from the window. Now."

I rolled out from under the bed and across the floor. Eight seconds later the window above my bed blew in. A branch the size of a canoe came through the glass and drove itself into my mattress, right where my head had been.

Tim has been right about every danger since. He has never once been wrong. He's also never once been calm, so I can't tell you which one's worse. Last month he made me cry in a grocery store, because the milk was one day from expiring and he'd decided that meant we were being hunted.

(It was just the milk.)

(He was still right that I shouldn't buy it.)

NEWT

Newt came next, a year later, because of a birthday party.

It wasn't my birthday. It was a classmate's. My mother made me go, because she wanted me to have a normal childhood, and you can't blame her for that; she just didn't know that for me a children's party is a hostile environment. Balloons screech. Paper hats scratch. Eleven kids all talk at once, in different pitches, and somebody's mom has put a song on repeat, and a kid I barely knew grabbed my arm to pull me toward a game, and the touch went through me like I'd been dropped in a bucket of ice and static.

I stood in the corner and I couldn't get out of my own skin.

And a calm, slightly nerdy voice said: "You know that wasn't actually a touch."

I stared at the floor.

"A touch is an illusion. When his hand met your arm, the electrons in the outer shells of his atoms repelled the electrons in the outer shells of yours. They never made contact. Nothing ever touches anything. All of it is just fields pushing on fields."

"...What?"

"The universe is ninety-nine point nine nine percent empty space. Nobody has ever touched you in your whole life. Not once. You're safe. You're basically a ghost standing in a room of ghosts."

And something loosened. I can't explain why a fact about electromagnetism made a room full of screaming children bearable, but it did. It gave the noise a shape. It put distance back between me and the world, a thin, sparkling, physical-law-shaped distance, and I could breathe inside it.

I named him Newt because I'd just read about Newton, and I was ten, and I thought it was funny to cut it off at the t.

Newt has gotten me through every exam, every lecture, and every panic attack I've ever had. He is also physically incapable of letting any situation pass without explaining it, including situations where explanation is the worst possible thing. He once began a lecture on the Pauli exclusion principle while a man was holding a knife to my throat. The man listened. He was impressed. He gave me his wallet back and apologized.

(That one's a long story. It's also not true. But Newt believes it is, and honestly it's a better story than the real one.)

REX

Rex showed up when I was eleven.

His name is easy, because he picked it himself. I never named him. He just arrived one day behind a boy named Daichi, who was a head taller than me and wanted my lunch, and who had cornered me behind the gym with a friend holding my backpack. I couldn't speak. My throat was a locked door. I stood there with my mouth open, staring at Daichi's shoes, and I knew that in about three seconds I was going to say nothing and get my lunch taken and my arm twisted. Then a voice like a drill sergeant said, in a tone with no room in it whatsoever:

"Look him in the eye. Say 'No.' Say it like you mean it."

I said, "No."

I said it exactly like that, flat, loud, and so cold that the boy next to Daichi took half a step back. Daichi blinked. He had been ready for a lot of things, and none of them were a very short eleven-year-old telling him no with the dead calm of a man announcing a hostage execution.

"Good," said the voice. "Now take your backpack and walk away. Don't run. Don't look back. Walk."

I walked. They didn't follow. Daichi never touched me again, and for the rest of that year, when the kids passed me in the hallway, they gave me a little extra room.

I thought: I want him around.

Rex doesn't ask. Rex tells. He's the voice that orders my coffee and tells the barista my name. He's the reason I can send an email to a professor without rewriting it nine times. He's also the reason that, during my sophomore year, I once told an entire lecture hall that the professor's grading rubric was "a participation trophy for adults," and was then asked to leave.

"It was accurate," Rex said, afterward.

"It was the tone," Newt said.

"The tone was accurate too," Rex said.

FAB

Fab I don't remember arriving.

That's strange, since he is the least forgettable thing about me. I think I was twelve or thirteen, at the age when the other kids had worked out a way to talk to each other, and a way to talk to adults, and a way to talk to people they liked, and I hadn't worked out any of those. I'd learned my way around teachers by memorizing what they wanted and giving it to them. Everyone else, I just didn't have a script for.

And then, one afternoon at the end of a school day, a teacher I was terrified of said, "Kaito, can I see you for a moment?" and before the panic hit, a voice like warm champagne said:

"Oh, honey. Smile. Tilt the head a little. Ask her how her weekend was and mean it. She's lonely. Look at the ring finger. She's lonelier than she lets on."

I did. She was. We talked for four minutes about her cat. She gave me an extension on a project, and she gave me a piece of candy, and I walked out of the room feeling like I'd managed to pick a lock.

"You're welcome," said the voice, "and by the way, the boy by the water fountain is incredible. Just saying."

"I'm straight," I told it, in my head.

"I know, sweetie," it said. "I'm not."

I called him Fab because he said the word "fabulous" eleven times in the first hour and I decided that was his name. He is glitter and charm and unbothered confidence and every joke that doesn't need to be explained to anyone. He's the part of me that can walk into a room and be liked. He's also the reason that, at a job interview for a campus library position, I leaned across the desk and told the supervisor that his tie was "doing a lot of work for him," which turned out to be the kindest thing anyone had said to him in a year, and I got the job.

He's also, and I cannot stress this enough, never once been subtle about a good-looking man. Not once. I live in a body where every handsome stranger on the street is greeted, internally, by a small fabulous voice sighing like a Victorian lady fainting on a couch.

I don't mind. I like that part of me. I just wish he'd stop commenting on the bus driver.

ROMEO

Romeo is the newest, and I'd like it noted that I didn't want him.

He turned up in the spring of my first year of university, a month after my eighteenth birthday. (For the record, I'm putting the age here because I'm going to be telling you a lot of things about Romeo and I want it to be crystal clear: he's an adult, and so is everyone he's ever embarrassed me in front of.) There was a girl in the library. She worked the front desk. She was tall, with ink on her fingers and her hair tied up with a pencil, and she looked up at me one day and said, "Hi, can I help you find something?"

I couldn't find a word. I stood there like a vending machine with no power.

And a warm, hopeful, dumb, lovestruck voice in my head said: "Oh, wow."

That was it. That was all he said. Oh, wow. And I felt my face go red from the collar up, and a whole new department of me opened for business, one that had never, ever been consulted about anything. Romeo was a flood. He was everything I'd never let myself feel, delivered at once, all the way. He wanted to say hello. He wanted to say a hundred things. He wanted to give the girl at the desk his whole life savings and a poem. He saw her shoes and nearly passed out.

"Her feet," he said, in a whisper like a man seeing a cathedral. "Look at her feet."

I don't know where that came from. I swear to you. He showed up fully formed, with that one particular obsession, with no explanation, and I've given up trying to find the origin story. Some people love sunsets. Some people love old cars. Some people look at a woman's arch and see a cathedral. I didn't make that choice. I'm not sure I'd have made it if I had a choice. But he's not going anywhere, and he's not hurting anyone, and he's, frankly, sweet about it. He just has a very specific thing. A special interest. I know a thing or two about those.

He's the reason I can't talk to girls. He's also the reason I even want to. Without Romeo, I'd be a man made of numbers who's never once wondered what it'd be like to be wanted. With him, I wonder about it, constantly, and pointlessly, and it makes me a little more human every time. It's an expensive trade.

"Their arches," Romeo sighed, from somewhere in the dark, right on cue.

"I SAID I WON'T DISCUSS IT," I told my dorm-room ceiling.

That's the five.

Rex, who decides. Fab, who charms. Newt, who explains. Tim, who warns. Romeo, who wants.

I'll tell you what a doctor never did: she never asked me whether I liked them. She asked whether they were "a symptom," and I said no, and she wrote something on her clipboard in a way that suggested she didn't believe me. But I made them. I built them like you'd build a toolkit when you've been dropped into a house without any tools. Each of them handles something I can't. And every time I use one, it works. Not because I'm crazy. Because I'm resourceful.

They're not what's wrong with me. They're what I did about it.

I got dressed. Hoodie, jeans, the same pair of beat-up sneakers. I checked my bag three times: laptop, notes, charger, half an egg sandwich. The phone sat at eleven percent, because I'd forgotten to charge it. Rex told me to leave. Tim told me the stairwell might be on fire. Newt gave me a lecture about the stairwell being, in all likelihood, completely fine. And Fab, taking in my appearance, said "Honey, we're doing what with that hair?"

I left.

The Walk

The campus was gray and washed out, with water standing on the paths. Everybody was in groups: clusters of friends under shared umbrellas, laughing at jokes I'd never hear. I kept my head down. Nobody looked at me. That's actually the arrangement I prefer, if I'm honest. Being unseen is easier than being misread.

A girl from my statistics class came the other way, a soft-spoken girl named Mio who wore flower-print raincoats. She lifted her hand and waved.

I froze. That's what I do. My brain, for a second, simply goes offline, and a log runs across the screen of my mind: wave detected; response required; response unknown.

"Wave back," Tim said. "Wave back. Wave back. WAVE BACK."

"Don't," said Rex. "Nod. Walk away like you don't care."

"Ooh, she's cute," said Fab. "Say hi, honey, she's looking at you."

"Look at her shoes," said Romeo, a worshipper at the altar. "Her sandals. In this weather. She's wearing sandals in the rain. She's brave. Kaito, her—"

"Hey," I said to Mio, in a voice like a man being strangled. "Uh. Nice sandals."

She gave me a very strange look and kept walking.

"Statistically," Newt said, "that went poorly."

"You said sandals," Tim sobbed.

"He said sandals," Romeo said, with the deep quiet pride of a father at a graduation.

"I'm so tired," I told the rain.

The Exam

I had a statistics exam at nine. I finished it in thirty-two minutes, which is, for the record, nothing special. I could have finished in twenty if Newt hadn't kept interrupting.

"Question six is trivial," he said. "Entropy always increases, and so do the number of points we're going to lose if you don't stop doodling."

"Hand it in," said Rex. "Walk out. Be confident."

"But what if we're wrong?" said Tim. "What if it's a trick? What if it's a trick question and there's a hidden number and we've missed it and we're going to fail and be homeless?"

"Is the proctor single?" said Fab.

"Is the girl next to us wearing open-toe?" said Romeo.

"Please," I whispered, to the exam paper. "One hour. Just give me one hour."

Silence. Nothing. A beat of something that might be the voices holding a vote. Then, from Rex, almost gently: "...Fine. One hour."

I finished, and I stood up and handed in the exam, and I started walking toward the door. And somewhere in the back of the room, a boy said, low, to someone else, in a voice built to be overheard:

"...Freak."

I heard it. Of course I heard it. I hear everything; that's half the problem. My shoulders dropped an inch. The hallway door felt about forty feet farther away than it had been a second ago.

For a long beat, the five of them said nothing at all.

Then Tim, very softly: "We heard that."

"Ignore them," said Rex. It wasn't an order. For once in his whole existence, it came out as a request.

"Honey," said Fab, with all the warmth in the world, "they're jealous."

"Statistically," said Newt, "geniuses are always misunderstood. It's the one thing the data's actually consistent about."

"Hey," said Romeo, quiet and kind and entirely without a joke in him, "we've got you."

I didn't answer. I nodded once, to nobody, in a hallway, and pushed the door open.

That's why the five exist. I know it sounds sappy. I'd like to say it in a cooler way. But that's the reason: because on the days when everybody else in the world has decided what I am, there are still five voices that have never once agreed with them.

The Roof

By evening the rain had stopped. I took my egg sandwich up to the roof of the library, where nobody goes, and I sat on the edge of an air vent with my legs hanging over a ten-meter drop and ate it in small, orderly bites, watching the sky go the color of a bruise.

The voices were quiet. That almost never happens. I got a full ten minutes of silence, and it felt like holding something fragile in both hands.

"I made you up because I needed company," I said, finally, to the air. It came out more honest than I'd planned. "But if I'm being honest, you were the only ones who ever listened."

A pause.

"Aw," said Fab. "That's sweet."

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm making it so weird."

"Quit it," said Rex, in a voice that suggested he'd been about to say something similar and had been robbed of the moment.

"I love you guys," said Tim, sniffling.

"Does anyone else smell cheese?" said Romeo.

I smiled. Small and tired and a little broken around the edges, but real. "Yeah," I said. "I know."

I lay back on the concrete. The sky was a dark, deep purple, and the first stars were coming out in the cracks between the clouds. I thought about what I had waiting for me: an exam tomorrow, a presentation, a group project with three people who'd already stopped replying to my messages. I did the math. The odds of surviving tomorrow came out pretty good.

"Statistically," I told the sky, "I'm going to be fine."

"Technically, yes," said Newt.

And then Tim, quietly, in a voice I hadn't heard from him in a long time, a voice that wasn't scared of the dark so much as certain about something inside it:

"...Hey. Something feels off."

I sat up. "Tim?"

"Nothing," he said. "Probably nothing."

I looked at the sky. At the empty, ordinary, purple sky. A plane blinked across it, red and slow.

For a single frame of a second, high above the campus, a thin ring of white light flickered in the air like a hair on a lens.

Then it was gone.

I didn't see it. Nobody saw it. Tim felt it, and Tim has never been wrong in his life, and I should have listened, and I didn't.

I finished my sandwich.

It was the last one I'd have for a long time.
